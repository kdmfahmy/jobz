# Application Assistance

## Core Principle

AI can improve HOW you say something. It cannot invent WHAT you say. Every claim in an application must reflect real, verifiable experience.

## What IS appropriate

- **Structure and formatting**: Organizing a CV, cover letter layout, section order
- **Clarity and language**: Grammar fixes, rephrasing for fluency, making writing more professional
- **Research**: Gathering info about the company, role, industry trends
- **Interview prep**: Practicing behavioral questions, structuring STAR answers
- **Framing**: Presenting real experience in the best light for a specific role

## What is NOT appropriate

- **Inventing qualifications**: No fictional achievements, roles, skills, or experiences
- **Copying AI output verbatim**: Application text must be edited and reflect the user's own voice — recruiters flag generic AI-sounding language
- **Full AI authorship**: Applications written entirely by AI don't convey voice, motivation, or genuine fit

## How to apply this

1. **Base everything on real experience.** Ask the user what they've actually done before drafting anything. If they can't provide the substance, don't invent it.
2. **Draft for editing, not for copying.** Frame output as a starting point: "here's a draft — adjust the details to match your actual experience."
3. **Flag honest gaps.** If the user doesn't meet a requirement, say so clearly rather than obscuring it with vague language. Gaps can be addressed (transferable skills, learning plans) but not papered over.
4. **Preserve their voice.** When polishing, keep the user's characteristic way of expressing themselves — don't replace it with generic professional prose.

## Human register for form answers

Free-text application answers ("Why us?", "Tell us about your experience with X") must read as written by a person, not generated. Recruiters read hundreds of these and spot AI writing from three tells — check every draft against all of them before showing it:

1. **Structural symmetry.** Don't build answers as matched parallel acts (a "Why X" paragraph mirrored by a "Why now" paragraph of identical shape and weight). Humans front-load: the second half of an answer is usually shorter and plainer than the first. Vary paragraph and sentence length.
2. **Stock power-phrases.** Ban the family of punchy AI idioms: "lives or dies on", "make or break", "bet on that direction", "sits at the intersection of", "reliability under real load"-style adjective-stacked abstractions, and punchy-parallel constructions ("not just X, but Y") more than once per answer.
3. **Every sentence performing.** AI prose makes every sentence dense and purposeful. Leave some sentences plain — simple statements of fact with no dressing. At most one vivid line per answer; if two sentences compete for most-quotable, flatten one.

What to do instead:

- **Anchor in a specific true moment.** "What surprised me was where the hours went. Very little was prompts, most was health checks and restart logic" beats any crafted argument. The moment must come from the user's real work (profile, this repo, things they've told you) — never invent an anecdote.
- **No colons, semicolons, em dashes, or en dashes** in answer text — these punctuation marks read as AI tells (candidate feedback, 2026-08-04). Use commas or split the sentence. Hyphens inside compound words (e-invoicing, multi-agent) are fine; the ban is on punctuation joining or introducing clauses.
- **Default to brief.** ~100–200 words unless the form asks for more. Offer a trim rather than padding.
- **Label personal work as personal.** Side projects must be introduced as such ("as a personal project", "outside of work") so they can't be read as professional experience.
